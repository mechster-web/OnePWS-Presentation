# Storytelling Director Guide

## Narrative Philosophy

The OnePWS Control Room Experience should feel like one continuous premium documentary, not a sequence of chapters. Every scene exists to create one question, guide one discovery, prove one idea and hand the audience into the next question.

The governing rule:

**Never explain everything. Create curiosity, answer it, then raise the next question.**

The presenter is not reading content. The presenter is guiding a room of decision-makers through a controlled revelation: modern operations are complex, people remain central, furniture alone is not enough, the environment must respond, intelligence must support the operator, and proof must show OnePWS can actually deliver.

## Master Question Chain

1. Why are modern control rooms under pressure?
2. What does the operator actually need?
3. Why is furniture alone not enough?
4. What changes human performance?
5. How should the environment respond?
6. What if every system worked together?
7. Can intelligent systems support without replacing the operator?
8. Can this be engineered and built?
9. Can OnePWS manufacture it consistently?
10. Has this already been proven?
11. What does the future control room become?
12. What should we design together next?

## Scene Pacing

Use a repeating documentary rhythm:

1. Question: one sentence.
2. Discovery: visual or interactive reveal.
3. Proof: source, product, system, project or human logic.
4. Transition: raise the next question.

No scene should allow more than 20 seconds without curiosity, decision, movement or discovery. Breathing moments are allowed, but they must feel intentional: silence, reflection, or a confident pause after a reveal.

## Presenter Conversation Guide

The presenter should sound like a guide, not a narrator reading captions.

Use these habits:

- Ask the question before revealing the answer.
- Pause after memory moments.
- Invite customer examples after operator-pressure and architecture scenes.
- Say “for your room, this would need to be engineered” whenever a technical boundary matters.
- Let proof breathe; do not stack credentials.
- When a customer asks a technical question, open the relevant technical layer rather than over-explaining.
- Treat optional branches as conversational turns, not detours.

## Timing Recommendations

- Opening: 60-90 seconds in full mode; 15-25 seconds in condensed mode.
- Problem setup: 2-4 minutes.
- Human-performance discovery: 3-5 minutes.
- Product / room build: 4-6 minutes.
- Intelligent operations: 4-6 minutes.
- Evidence and proof: 4-7 minutes, depending on audience.
- Final synthesis and call to action: 60-120 seconds.

Every chapter should produce a meaningful discovery within 20 seconds and a meaningful event within 30 seconds.

## Recommended Silence

Silence is strongest:

- Opening first frame.
- Before “it must understand.”
- After complete room assembly.
- Before operator confirmation.
- After project proof appears.
- During final room settling.

## Narration Density

- Opening: low, poetic.
- Problem: low-medium, scenario-led.
- Human performance: medium, explanatory.
- Product: low, visual-led.
- Architecture: low-medium, spatial.
- Intelligence: medium, status-led.
- Evidence: low, precise.
- Closing: low, reflective.

Avoid reading statistics aloud unless the presenter is deliberately answering a credibility question.

## Interaction Density

- High: journey selection, operator challenges, console portfolio, complete ecosystem, incident response, project portfolio.
- Medium: ergonomic methodology, sightline comfort, architectural systems, manufacturing-quality, certifications.
- Low: opening, company continuity, final closing.

Low interaction is acceptable only when visual, silence or proof carries the moment.

## Story Continuity Validation

Validation checklist:

- Every chapter has a governing question.
- Every chapter ends by raising the next question.
- Every major concept appears at least twice: operator, room, console, intelligence, proof, future.
- Memory moments are limited and spaced.
- Proof appears after possibility, not before the need is understood.
- The final closing connects all earlier concepts.
- Presenter discussion points are built into natural pauses.

## Chapter Connection Report

Strongest connections:

- Opening to mission context: room awakening leads into why the room matters.
- Operator pressure to human-centred design: problem naturally creates design philosophy.
- Console to complete ecosystem: product becomes system anchor.
- Complete ecosystem to intelligence: room state becomes signal path.
- Incident response to ergonomics/architecture/proof: response story creates need for engineering credibility.
- Evidence to why OnePWS: proof consolidates into buying confidence.
- Why OnePWS to closing: capability becomes next design action.

Connections needing presenter care:

- `company-at-a-glance` and `group-and-growth` appear early in the global chapter order. Use them briefly or route them later through credibility mode when the audience is not ready for proof.
- `ergonomic-methodology` after `incident-response` can feel like a technical rewind. Presenter should frame it as “now let’s look at the method behind that response.”
- Certification details can feel static. Always open with “what risk does this reduce?” before showing proof.

## Final Emotional Payoff

The ending should make the audience realise the story was never about separate products or services.

The operator needed support.

The desk organised the work.

The architecture shaped focus.

The technology connected attention.

AI assisted without replacing judgement.

Manufacturing made it buildable.

Projects made it credible.

Evidence made it trustworthy.

The future is not a slide after the presentation. It is the customer’s next control room.
